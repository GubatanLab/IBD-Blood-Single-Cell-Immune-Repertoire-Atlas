# MultiNicheNet analysis of T-helper–B-cell coupling

## Analysis scope

MultiNicheNet v2.1.0 was applied to paired participant-level CD4 and B-lineage single-cell RNA-seq objects using the official human NicheNet ligand–receptor network and ligand–target matrix. The analysis included 139,687 CD4 cells and 106,793 B-lineage cells after focusing on Tfh, Th17/Th1–Th17, Treg, naive/transitional B, conventional memory B, atypical memory B, IgM plasma, and switched plasma compartments. Ligand–receptor testing used raw counts and edgeR pseudobulk differential expression, required at least five cells per participant–state and four participants per comparison group, and used a 0.25 log-fold-change threshold, 5% expression-fraction threshold, and 50% minimum sample prevalence. The primary analyses were CD inflamed versus noninflamed (116 participants), UC inflamed versus noninflamed (73 participants), CD versus UC restricted to shared acquisition series (151 participants), and diagnosis contrasts restricted to acquisition series S6 (42 participants). Acquisition series was included as a covariate where it was estimable.

The four analyses yielded 31,817 cross-lineage prioritized interaction records. A prespecified mechanistic panel identified 1,957 records involving CD40, APRIL/BAFF, TGF-β, MHC-II–CD4, CD80/CD86 costimulation, ICOS, ICAM-1, and TNF/lymphotoxin programs. Participant-level communication scores were then calculated only when both sender and receiver populations were present and integrated with T-helper programs, B-cell programs, bulk repertoire summaries, and BCR lineage features. Partial rank associations adjusted for clinical group, acquisition series where applicable, and CD4/B-cell sampling depth; multiplicity correction was performed across all tested score–outcome pairs within each analysis and communication direction.

## Main findings

B-cell-to-helper-T-cell antigen-presentation and costimulatory programs were the most reproducible signal. MHC-II–CD4 and CD86–CD28/CTLA4 interactions occurred in all four analyses and all six modeled contrasts. In UC inflammation, atypical-memory-B-cell HLA-DPB1–CD4 signaling to Tfh cells was the highest-ranked canonical interaction (priority score 0.894; overall rank 1). In the shared-series CD-versus-UC comparison, atypical-memory-B-cell HLA-DRA–CD4 signaling to Th17 cells was the highest-ranked interaction overall (score 0.884), accompanied by recurrent HLA-DPB1/DQB1/DRB5 signals and CD86 costimulation. In S6-only diagnosis models, CD was characterized by memory and atypical-memory B-cell CD86–CD28 signaling to Treg/Th17 cells (scores up to 0.871) together with MHC-II signals, whereas UC showed a prominent CD40-help module from Th17/Tfh cells to atypical-memory and naive/transitional B cells (maximum score 0.755). These results support a bidirectional circuit in which antigen-presenting B-cell states reinforce helper-T-cell activation while helper T cells provide survival and differentiation signals back to B cells. MHC-II–CD4 edges should be interpreted as an antigen-presentation-compatible transcriptional state, not evidence of a particular peptide–TCR interaction.

The dominant T-helper-to-B-cell programs were context dependent. In inflamed CD, Treg TGFB1–TGFBR3 signaling to IgM plasma cells was the second-ranked cross-lineage interaction overall (score 0.856), with ligand-target support including SMAD7 and TIMP2 in relevant TGF-β models. Th17/Tfh CD40LG–CD40 signaling was also present and its inferred participant-level score correlated with the B-cell antigen-presentation program in CD inflammation (adjusted rho 0.511, 95% approximate CI 0.354–0.639, FDR 2.2 × 10⁻6) and in the CD-versus-UC analysis (rho 0.427, CI 0.283–0.553, FDR 8.5 × 10⁻6). In S6 diagnosis contrasts, Th17-derived TNFSF13B signaling to TNFRSF13B/C/17 was prominent in CD, including switched plasma cells. B-cell MHC-II communication scores correlated with Tph/Tfh-help and Th17/Treg programs, while B-to-T TGF-β scores were inversely related to those programs. These associations provide coherent transcriptomic triangulation but are not independent validation because the communication and program scores derive from the same single-cell expression measurements.

Coupling to structural repertoire features was substantially weaker. Of the tested BCR switching, SHM, lineage-expansion, and lineage-divergence outcomes, only the CD-selected top-25 cross-lineage composite versus BCR IgA fraction survived FDR correction (rho −0.285, n = 120, FDR 0.020). Thus, the strongest mechanistic evidence concerns coordinated cell-state programs rather than a direct link to somatic diversification. Clone-aware within-participant comparisons provided additional nuance: expanded Treg and Th17 clonotypes expressed more TGFBR3 than singleton clonotypes (median normalized-expression differences 0.362 and 0.221; both FDR 0.023), whereas expanded naive/transitional B clonotypes showed lower TNFRSF13C, ICOSLG, CD40, CD80, TNF, and LTB expression. This argues against a simple model in which all expanded B-cell clones are uniformly hyperresponsive; instead, receptor expansion and communication state appear partially separable.

## Interpretation and limitations

Together, the findings support a mechanistic model of circulating bidirectional T-helper–B-cell coupling in IBD: B-cell antigen-presentation/costimulatory states track helper-T-cell activation, while TGF-β, CD40, and APRIL/BAFF inputs partition inflammatory, regulatory, and plasma-cell-directed responses. The balance differs by disease context—MHC-II/Tfh coupling is especially strong in inflamed UC, MHC-II/Th17 and CD86 costimulation distinguish CD in shared-series comparisons, and Treg-to-IgM-plasma TGF-β signaling is prominent in inflamed CD. These axes are candidates for validation by flow cytometry or CITE-seq, paired blood–tissue mapping, spatial assays, and receptor-expression experiments.

MultiNicheNet infers communication from expression, differential state, prior ligand–receptor knowledge, and predicted ligand-target activity. It does not establish physical cell contact, signaling flux, peptide specificity, or causal direction. The B and T cells were collected from the same participants but were not experimentally paired, and peripheral blood cannot resolve tissue-localized niches. Switched plasma cells failed the primary differential-expression gate in several adjusted analyses because of sparse coverage and should remain a sensitivity endpoint. The diagnosis design also required acquisition-series restriction because series and diagnosis were partially confounded. These findings are therefore best presented as mechanistic hypotheses that extend the repertoire atlas, with external and tissue-level validation required.
